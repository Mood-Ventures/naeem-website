Nervous System Regulation: How High Performers Stay Clear Under Pressure

Home / Blog

November 23, 2026

Last week I wrote about what a high performance coach works on, and the first thing on that list was state. Before that, I wrote about how your brain changes. Myelination strengthens the paths you use. Synaptic pruning clears the ones you don't.

This week is about the system that decides which of those paths shows up when it counts: your nervous system.

You can know exactly what you want to do in a hard conversation, a big pitch or a tense moment at home. Then your body reads the moment as a threat, and an older pattern takes over. The fix is rarely more willpower. It's state, and state is something you can learn to work with. That skill is called nervous system regulation.

What Is Nervous System Regulation?

Your nervous system is constantly scanning for one question: am I safe right now? Based on the answer, it shifts your body into the mode it thinks you need.

Nervous system regulation is your ability to move between those modes and come back to a calm, clear baseline after something activates you. A regulated system still gets fired up. It just doesn't get stuck there.

Think of it like a thermostat rather than a light switch. The goal isn't to be calm all the time. The goal is to be able to turn the heat up when you need it and bring it back down when the moment has passed.

Sympathetic vs. Parasympathetic: Your Two Gears

The part of your nervous system that runs in the background without you thinking about it is called the autonomic nervous system. It has two main branches, and it helps to picture them as two gears.

  • The sympathetic branch is your accelerator. It drives the fight or flight response: heart rate goes up, breathing gets faster and higher in the chest, muscles get ready to act, and attention narrows onto the threat.
  • The parasympathetic branch is your brake. It's often called "rest and digest." Heart rate slows, breathing deepens, digestion and recovery switch back on, and attention widens again. The vagus nerve carries a lot of this calming signal.

Neither gear is good or bad. You want the accelerator before a big presentation or a sprint. You want the brake when you're trying to sleep, listen to your partner or think through a complex decision. Trouble starts when the accelerator gets stuck on.

Why Your State Decides Which Habit Shows Up

Here's where this connects to everything I've written about habits.

When your body is in a strong threat response, the parts of the brain involved in planning, judgment and self-control tend to work less well. Research on the prefrontal cortex points in this direction: under heavy pressure, the brain leans toward faster, more automatic, more habitual responses.

In plain language: when you're activated, you don't rise to your intentions. You fall back on your most practiced pattern.

That's why the new habit you're building can look solid on a calm Tuesday and disappear in a tense Thursday meeting. The habit didn't fail. Your state picked the older, better-worn path. In over 1,000 talks I've watched this play out in rooms full of smart, capable people. The people who change for good usually aren't the ones with the most willpower. They learn to manage their state first.

Signs of a Dysregulated Nervous System

A dysregulated nervous system is one that has trouble returning to baseline. It might stay revved up, or it might swing into shutdown and numbness. Some common signs high performers describe:

  • A short fuse with your team or family that wasn't there before
  • Feeling wired at night and tired in the morning
  • Shallow, fast breathing you only notice when you stop
  • Rushing, over-explaining or talking over people in conversations
  • Struggling to switch off on weekends or vacation
  • Feeling flat or checked out even when things are going well

Everyone has days like this. The signal to pay attention to is when it becomes your normal.

The Window of Tolerance

Psychiatrist Dan Siegel described a useful idea called the window of tolerance. Inside your window, you can feel strong emotion and still think, listen and choose. Above it, you tip into fight or flight. Below it, you drop into shutdown.

Two things are worth knowing. First, your window gets narrower when you're short on sleep, under pressure for weeks or running on caffeine and adrenaline. Second, it can get wider with practice. Every time you notice activation and bring yourself back, you're training that return path. That's the same use-it-or-lose-it principle from myelination and pruning, applied to your state.

How to Regulate Your Nervous System: 7 Simple Practices

None of these are complicated. The power is in doing them often, in small doses, before you need them.

1. Make your exhale longer than your inhale

Slow, longer exhales are one of the most direct ways to tap the brake. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four, and out slowly for a count of six to eight. A few rounds is enough to notice a shift.

2. Try a few cyclic sighs

Take a full breath in through your nose, add a second short sip of air at the top, then let it all out slowly through your mouth. A Stanford study published in 2023 found that five minutes a day of this kind of breathing improved mood and reduced physiological arousal, and did better on mood than mindfulness meditation in that study.

3. Name what you feel

Put a word on it: "I'm tense." "I'm frustrated." "I feel rushed." Naming the state moves you from being inside it to observing it. It's small, and it creates a gap between the trigger and the old reaction.

4. Orient to the room

Slowly look around and let your eyes land on a few things: the door, the window, something blue. This simple act of orienting tells your body you've checked the space and it's safe enough to stand down.

5. Move your body

Emotion comes from motion. A short walk, a few shoulder rolls or shaking out your hands helps your body complete the stress response it started, instead of carrying it into the next meeting.

6. Change the question you're asking

Your nervous system responds to the questions your mind asks. "What if this goes wrong?" keeps the accelerator down. "What's one thing I can do right now?" gives your body something to act on. Language is one of the fastest ways to shift state.

7. Protect your recovery

Sleep, daylight in the morning and a real wind-down at night all widen your window of tolerance. Recovery is part of performance.

Practice Your State Before You Need It

The biggest mistake I see is trying to regulate only in the middle of the storm. That's like trying to learn to swim while you're drowning.

Pick one practice from the list. Do it three times a day when nothing is wrong: before you open your laptop, before lunch and before you walk in the door at home. You're building the path while the stakes are low, so it's there when the stakes are high.

That's how you change which habit shows up. You practice the new behavior and the state that makes it possible.
