What a High Performance Coach Actually Works On (And Why It Starts in the Nervous System)

Home / Blog

November 16, 2026

Most people who look for a high performance coach already perform well. They run companies, lead teams, close deals and keep a dozen plates spinning. The problem usually isn’t a lack of ability or information. It’s that the way they operate has started to cost more than it should.

Maybe decisions that used to feel clean now feel heavy. Maybe there’s a short fuse in meetings that wasn’t there two years ago. Maybe the calendar is full and the work is good, but the person doing it feels flat. None of that shows up on a dashboard, and all of it affects results.

So what does a high performance coach actually do? After more than 15 years studying human potential, working as a coach, NLP trainer and hypnotist, and speaking to audiences around the world, here is how I think about it.

Performance Is a State Before It’s a Strategy

We tend to treat performance as a thinking problem. Get the right plan, the right priorities, the right framework, and output will follow. Plans matter. But plans are executed by a nervous system, and that nervous system has its own agenda.

When your body reads a situation as threatening (a tense board meeting, a big pitch, a hard conversation with a cofounder), it shifts toward protection. Attention narrows. Options seem to shrink. You default to whatever pattern has kept you safe before, which might be over-controlling, over-explaining, going quiet or pushing harder.

That shift isn’t a character flaw. It’s biology doing its job. The coaching question is whether you can notice it early and choose your response, instead of letting the old pattern run the room.

This is why a lot of my coaching work starts with managing state. Before we talk about strategy, we look at how you arrive. How do you breathe when you walk into a difficult meeting? What does your body do when someone challenges you? What is your recovery like after a long, demanding day? Small, practical changes here tend to make everything downstream easier.

Language Shapes What You Can See

The second area is language, and specifically the language you use with yourself.

One of the simplest distinctions from NLP is the difference between a problem frame and an outcome frame. A problem frame asks: what’s wrong, why is it happening, whose fault is it? An outcome frame asks: what do I want, what would that look like, what’s the next step toward it?

Both have their place. You need to understand a problem to solve it. But many high performers live almost entirely in the problem frame. They are excellent at spotting risk, which is part of why they succeeded, and they never switch modes. The mind keeps scanning for what could go wrong, and the body stays braced.

A good coach listens for which frame you’re in and helps you move between them on purpose. Often the shift is as small as changing one question. “Why does this keep happening?” becomes “What would I need to believe or do for this to go differently next time?” The facts are the same. What you can see changes.

Better Questions, Better Answers

Your brain is very good at answering the questions you give it. Ask “Why am I so behind?” and it will find plenty of reasons. Ask “What is the one thing that would make today a win?” and it goes looking for something else entirely.

Part of high performance coaching is auditing the questions you ask on autopilot. Most of us have a few that repeat all day without our noticing. A few examples worth checking in yourself:

  • When something goes wrong, is your first question about blame or about learning?
  • When you set a goal, do you ask what you want, or only what you want to avoid?
  • When you’re tired, do you ask what you need, or how to push through?

Changing a habitual question feels minor. Over weeks and months, it tends to change the quality of your thinking more than almost any productivity system.

Beliefs Are Hypotheses, Not Facts

Underneath state and language sit beliefs. Beliefs about what you’re capable of, what other people expect, what success requires, and what happens if you slow down.

Many of these were formed early and worked well for a while. “If I outwork everyone, I’ll be fine” can build a career. It can also make rest feel dangerous twenty years later.

I find it useful to treat beliefs the way a scientist treats a hypothesis. Not as something to defend, but as something to test. What evidence supports it? What evidence doesn’t? What would you do differently if a more useful belief were also true? This is where tools from NLP and hypnosis can help, because they work with the parts of the mind that don’t change just because you understand something intellectually.

If you’re curious about why change takes repetition and not only insight, I wrote about the biology behind it in What Is Myelination?.

What Working With a High Performance Coach Looks Like

Every coach works differently, so it’s worth asking any coach you consider how they approach it. In my practice, the work usually includes:

  • Clarity on the outcome. What specifically do you want to be different in three, six and twelve months, and how will you know?
  • Noticing your patterns under pressure. Where does your state shift, and what does it cost you?
  • Practical state tools. Breath, attention, movement and recovery practices you can actually use between meetings.
  • Language and question work. Catching the frames and questions that limit you, and rehearsing better ones.
  • Belief updating. Finding the assumptions driving behavior and testing whether they still serve you.
  • Repetition. Change gets built through practice between sessions, not only through conversations during them.

The goal isn’t to turn you into someone else. It’s to remove the friction between who you are at your best and how you show up on a hard Tuesday.

How to Choose a High Performance Coach

A few questions worth asking before you commit to anyone, including me:

  • What is their training? Credentials don’t guarantee quality, but they show commitment to the craft. I’m an ICF-accredited coach, and I’ve trained in NLP and hypnosis.
  • Do they work with the body or only the mind? Performance under pressure is physical. A purely intellectual approach tends to stall.
  • Can they explain their method plainly? If it sounds like magic, be careful.
  • Do they understand your world? Leading a company has specific pressures. You want someone who has spent time around them.
  • Do you feel more like yourself after talking to them? Chemistry matters more than most people admit.

A Small Place to Start

If you want to try one thing this week, try this. Before your next important meeting, take sixty seconds. Slow your exhale so it’s longer than your inhale. Then ask yourself one question: “What outcome do I want from this conversation?” Not what you want to avoid. What you want.

Notice whether the meeting feels different. Most people find it does.
