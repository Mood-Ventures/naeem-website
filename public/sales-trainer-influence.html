What a Great Sales Trainer Teaches About Influence That Scripts Never Will

Home / Blog

November 2, 2026

Most sales training focuses on what to say. Openers, objection handlers, closing lines, talk tracks. Those have value. But anyone who has sold for a while knows two people can use the exact same script and get completely different results.

The difference is influence. Not the pushy kind. The quiet kind that makes a buyer feel safe enough to think out loud, honest enough to tell you what's really in the way, and clear enough to decide. That's the layer I focus on as a sales trainer, and it comes from years as a speaker, coach, NLP trainer and hypnotist.

Here's how I break influence down, and how you can build it.

Influence Starts in Your Nervous System

A sales conversation is two nervous systems reading each other. Before your prospect processes a single word of your pitch, they've picked up your pace, your tone and whether you seem relaxed or desperate.

That's your first act of influence, and it happens before you open your mouth. When you're anxious about a number, speech speeds up, listening gets shallow, and you jump to solutions before the buyer finishes explaining the problem. Buyers feel that pressure, and most people respond to pressure by pulling back.

So the first thing I teach is managing state before and during calls:

  • Arrive on purpose. Take a minute before the call. Slow your exhale. Put your attention on the person, not your quota.
  • Notice your tells. Everyone has a signal that their state is slipping: talking faster, over-explaining, filling silence. Learn yours.
  • Use the pause. A few seconds of silence after a question gives the buyer room to think. Most sellers rush to fill it.

A calm seller influences the whole room. People borrow your state. If you're steady, they get steady, and steady people make clear decisions.

Questions Are the Most Underrated Influence Tool

Buyers believe what they say far more than what you say. That makes questions the most powerful form of influence you have. A good question doesn't tell someone what to think. It points their attention, and attention shapes conclusions.

Weak questions gather facts: "How many people are on your team?" Strong questions move thinking: "What happens if this stays the way it is for another year?" or "What would need to be true for this to be an easy decision?"

A few principles I come back to:

  • Ask about outcomes, not only problems. Problems tell you what hurts. Outcomes tell you what they'll invest in.
  • Ask one question at a time. Stacked questions let the buyer answer the easiest one.
  • Follow the energy. When a buyer leans in on a topic, go deeper there instead of moving to your next question.

When the buyer reaches the answer themselves, you haven't persuaded them. You've helped them persuade themselves, and that decision sticks.

Frames: Where You Point the Conversation

One idea from NLP that changes sales conversations is the difference between a problem frame and an outcome frame. Frames are influence at the level of the whole conversation.

In a problem frame, you talk about what's broken, why it's broken and how bad it is. That's useful for building urgency. Stay there too long, though, and the buyer starts to feel heavy and defensive.

In an outcome frame, you talk about what they want, what it would look like and what it would make possible. This is where buyers start to picture the result, and where motivation comes from.

Skilled sellers move between the two deliberately. Understand the problem honestly, then help the buyer step into the outcome: "That makes sense. If we fast forward six months and this is solved, what's different for you?"

Whoever sets the frame shapes the decision. Set it with care.

Objections Are Beliefs, and Beliefs Don't Move Under Pressure

Many objections aren't really about price or timing. They're about a belief: "We tried something like this and it didn't work," "My team won't adopt it," or "This is too big a risk for me personally."

Arguing with a belief is the fastest way to lose influence. People defend beliefs, especially when they feel pushed. Curiosity works better. Ask what's behind the concern. Ask what happened last time. Ask what would need to be different for them to feel confident. Often the buyer talks themselves into a more useful belief, or tells you exactly what you need to address.

The same applies to you. Many salespeople carry beliefs that quietly cap their influence: "Following up is annoying," "Big clients won't take me seriously," "I'm not a natural closer." Part of good sales training is surfacing and testing those beliefs, because they shape behavior far more than any script.

Influence Is a Skill, So Train It Like One

A one-day workshop can be inspiring. Without practice afterward, most of it fades. Listening under pressure, asking a hard question calmly and holding a pause are built through repetition, like any skill. I wrote about the brain science behind that in What Is Myelination?.

That's why the training I find most useful includes role play, recorded calls, small daily drills and follow up. Less information, more reps. Influence grows the same way strength does.

What to Look for in a Sales Trainer

If you're choosing a sales trainer for yourself or your team, check for these:

  • Do they teach influence, or only process? Process matters, but people buy from people.
  • Do they build in practice? Ask how skills get reinforced after the session ends.
  • Do they understand your buyers? Selling to founders and executives is different from transactional sales.
  • Can they hold a room? Training is partly performance. I've given 1,000 talks and reached 50,000+ lives, and the energy in the room makes a real difference to what people keep.
  • Do they make your team better listeners? If a training only makes people better talkers, look elsewhere.

Try This on Your Next Call

Make one change. After the buyer describes their problem, don't pitch. Ask: "If this were solved, what would that make possible for you?" Then stay quiet and let them answer fully.

Notice how the conversation shifts when they describe the outcome in their own words. That's influence working the way it should: you guided, they decided.
