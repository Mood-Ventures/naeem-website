Train Your Mind Like Your Body: How High Performers Build Mind Strength

Home / Blog

November 9, 2026

You already train the parts of your life that feel measurable.

If you sell, you practice the conversation. If you speak, you rehearse the room. If you take care of your body, you do not wait until game day to see whether you are in shape.

A lot of high performers still treat the mind like a personality trait. They hope it shows up ready. Then a high-stakes call, a tense negotiation, or a public moment arrives, and the basics get hard. The mind stops helping and starts interfering.

That is not a talent problem. It is a training problem.

Last week I wrote about nervous system regulation: your state decides which habit shows up when it counts. This week is the next step. Once you can notice your state, you can train the mind that steers it.

I call that work mind strength.

What mind strength actually is

Mind strength is not a vague pep talk, and it is not the same thing as mental health support. In my Peak Humans conversation with former NFL quarterback and author Luke Falk (#224), we talked about the difference.

Mental health support matters when someone is hurting and needs help. Mind strength is the proactive side: the skills you build before the wall hits, the same way you lift, study film, or run a sales process before you need it live.

Luke wrote The Mind Strength Playbook around that idea. Confidence can be trained. Ownership can be trained. Returning to the present can be trained. None of it arrives overnight, and none of it stays sharp if you only pick it up in an emergency.

If myelination strengthens the paths you use, and synaptic pruning clears the ones you do not, mind strength is the daily decision to use the path you want under pressure.

When the mind gets in the way

Luke described a pattern a lot of high performers recognize, even if they have never taken a snap.

He trained his body. He put the work in. Then a big game or a big moment arrived, and the simplest things locked up. His mind became interference instead of an asset.

That is the expensive version of being untrained. You do not usually lose the high-stakes moment because you forgot the strategy. You lose it because the mind you did not practice takes over the body you did.

Luke's parents introduced him to sports psychologist Dr. Craig Manning. The lesson Luke took from that work is the heart of this week: if you work on your mind the way you work on your body, it gets stronger over time. It does not happen overnight. It happens through reps.

Own what you can control

One of the sharpest parts of that conversation was a lesson Luke learned from Coach Mike Leach.

Leach used to tell his quarterbacks that all sacks are on the quarterback. Luke has said himself that this is not literally true. A protection can break down. A lineman can get beat. Anyone who watches football can see that.

The coaching was pointing somewhere else. If you spend the play blaming other people, you only improve when they improve. You only get better when the circumstances are already perfect.

The better question is: what can I control?

Can I change the play? Can I get rid of the ball? Can I scramble? Can I check it down? If a receiver drops it, did I give him the right pace, the right location, the right amount of work together before the snap?

That translates out of football immediately.

In a sales conversation, the other person may not buy. You still own your preparation, your questions, your state, and the follow-up. In a leadership moment, you may not own the market or someone else's mood. You do own how quickly you return to what is actually in your hands.

Ownership is not self-blame. It is a narrower, more useful focus. It keeps your attention on the next rep you can take.

Start now, and get your shots up

Mind strength sounds important, which is exactly why people delay it. They wait for a quieter month, a better mood, or a clearer plan.

The more honest version is simpler: start now.

Mastery, in almost every field I work in, is reps. Time on the thing. Getting your shots up. You do not think your way into a stronger mind any more than you think your way into a stronger body. You practice when it is ordinary so the skill is available when it is not.

That can look like five minutes before you open the laptop. A longer exhale before you walk into a meeting. One question you ask yourself after a miss: "What part of that was mine to own?"

You do not need a perfect protocol to begin. You need the first rep.

The fuel you train with matters

There is another trap high performers fall into. They treat pressure, proving, and grinding as the only serious fuel.

Life is not that binary. You can want the result and still operate from a better place than threat. In a recent teaching reel I talked about duality — the mind loves this or that, good or bad, dark or light — and about entrepreneurs I have seen get more done when they work from "I am joyful, I am worthy" instead of from depletion.

That is not permission to go soft on standards. It is a reminder that the nervous system you bring to the rep becomes part of the rep. If every practice session is run on panic, you are training panic. If you can practice from a steadier, more generous fuel, you are training that too.

Last week's language still applies here: practice your state before you need it. Mind strength is what you are practicing for.

A simple way to train this week

Keep this small enough that you will actually do it.

  1. Pick one controllable. After any miss, ask: what part of that was mine? Preparation, recovery, the next conversation, the next rep. Leave the rest.
  2. Take one mental rep a day when nothing is wrong. A longer exhale. A written intention before the first meeting. Two minutes of reviewing what you will own today.
  3. Notice the fuel. If you only train when you feel behind, you are teaching your mind that urgency is the requirement. Add one session that starts from enoughness.
  4. Repeat. Strength is not a quote you read. It is a path you use until it is the one that shows up under pressure.

Your body already understands this. Your craft already understands this. The mind can learn it too.
