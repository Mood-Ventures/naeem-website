What Is Synaptic Pruning? How Your Brain Lets Go of What You Don’t Use

Home / Blog

October 26, 2026

Last week I wrote about myelination, the way your brain may add insulation to the pathways you use often. This week is the other half of that story.

Your brain doesn't only build. It also edits. It lets go of connections that aren't pulling their weight, and that process is called synaptic pruning.

When I first understood pruning, it changed how I coach. It explained something I had seen in a lot of rooms over 1,000 talks: people who wanted a new habit badly, but who kept feeding the old one every single day without noticing.

What Is Synaptic Pruning?

Your brain is made of neurons, and neurons talk to each other at tiny junctions called synapses. Every skill you have, every habit, every automatic reaction relies on networks of these connections.

Synaptic pruning is the process of removing synapses. In simple terms: the brain builds more connections than it needs, then trims the ones that aren't being used much, so the ones that remain can work better.

The picture I like is a gardener with a rose bush. A good gardener doesn't let every branch grow wild. They cut back the weak shoots so the strong ones get the light and the energy. The plant ends up healthier because of what was removed, not in spite of it.

You'll also see this called neural pruning or brain pruning. Same idea.

Why Would a Brain Throw Connections Away?

It sounds backwards at first. Wouldn't more connections mean a better brain?

Not quite. Early in life, the brain produces a huge surplus of synapses. Researchers have tracked this for decades by counting synapses in brain tissue at different ages, and the pattern is consistent: a big wave of growth early, followed by a long period of trimming through childhood and adolescence. The timing differs by region. Areas involved in planning, judgment and self-control are among the last to finish, with some research suggesting changes there continue into the twenties.

Pruning is a normal, healthy part of that development. A brain with every possible connection would be noisy. A brain that keeps the useful connections and clears the rest is faster and more efficient. Less clutter, clearer signal.

How the Brain Decides What to Keep

Here's the part that matters for anyone trying to grow. Pruning isn't random. A big factor in what survives appears to be activity.

Connections that are used often tend to be strengthened and kept. Connections that sit quiet are more likely to be weakened and removed. Neuroscientists have a famous shorthand for the first half of this: neurons that fire together, wire together. Pruning is the quieter second half. Connections that stop firing together tend to come apart.

Scientists have also discovered that the brain has its own cleanup crew. Immune cells in the brain called microglia help remove synapses, and research has identified molecular "tags" that help mark certain connections for removal. The details are still being worked out, and it's an active, fascinating area of research. But the big picture is clear enough to be useful: the brain is constantly making decisions about what to keep, and use is one of the strongest signals it listens to.

That's why you'll hear pruning summed up as use it or lose it. It's a simplification, but it's a helpful one.

Does Synaptic Pruning Happen in Adults?

This is the question I get most, so let me be careful with it.

The big, sweeping pruning that shapes the brain happens mostly during childhood and adolescence. As an adult, you're not going through that same dramatic developmental wave.

But your brain doesn't freeze. Throughout adult life, connections keep forming, strengthening, weakening and disappearing in response to what you do and what you learn. Scientists usually call this synaptic plasticity rather than pruning in the developmental sense, but the principle rhymes. The connections you use get support. The ones you neglect tend to fade.

Sleep seems to be part of this too. One leading idea in sleep research is that sleep helps the brain rebalance its connections after a day of learning, keeping what matters and turning down the rest. It's still being studied, but it's one more reason I tell clients that rest is part of practice, not a break from it.

So the honest answer is: adults don't prune the way teenagers do, but your brain is still editing itself, every day, based on how you live.

Pruning and Myelination: Two Sides of the Same Coin

If you read my guide to myelination, you'll see how these fit together.

  • Myelination may help the pathways you use often send signals faster and with better timing.
  • Pruning clears away connections that aren't being used, so the brain's resources go to the ones that are.

Daniel Coyle's book The Talent Code made the myelin side of this famous. He visited places that produced unusual numbers of high performers and found people practicing in a particular way: focused, at the edge of their ability, making mistakes and correcting them. His point was that how you practice shapes the circuits you build.

Pruning adds the other half of the lesson. It's not only about what you build. It's also about what you stop feeding.

What This Means for Changing a Habit

Most people try to change by adding something. A new routine, a new app, a new goal. That's fine. But pruning points to something we usually ignore: the old pattern is also being practiced.

Every time you check your phone first thing in the morning, you rehearse that path. Every time you jump in and solve the problem for your team instead of asking a question, you rehearse that path. Every time you tell yourself "I'm just not good at this," you rehearse that path too.

Your brain doesn't care about your intentions. It responds to your repetitions. That's both the challenge and the good news. If use keeps a connection strong, then reducing use gives the brain a reason to let it fade, and every time you choose the new response, you're casting a vote for the new path.

How to Use Synaptic Pruning to Change: 7 Practical Steps

You don't need a lab to work with this. Whether you're a founder learning to delegate, a leader changing how you show up in meetings, or anyone trying to drop an old habit or belief, here's how I'd apply it.

  1. Name the pattern you're feeding. Be specific. Not "I'm reactive," but "When someone pushes back in a meeting, I interrupt within five seconds." You can't stop rehearsing something you can't see.
  2. Find the trigger. Old patterns usually fire in the same situations. The first email of the day. A certain person's tone. Late at night on the couch. Knowing the cue gives you a moment to choose.
  3. Replace, don't just resist. Trying to "not do" something often keeps your attention on it. Pick a specific new response for the same trigger: pause and ask one question, take three slow breaths, put the phone in another room.
  4. Make the new rep small and frequent. Short, regular practice is a better bet than one heroic effort. Small daily reps give your brain a steady signal about what matters now.
  5. Lower the friction on the new path and raise it on the old one. Environment design helps. If the old habit takes effort and the new one is easy, you'll rehearse the new one more often.
  6. Protect your sleep. Since sleep appears to play a role in how the brain sorts and consolidates connections, treat it as part of your practice plan.
  7. Be patient with the timeline. Old patterns don't vanish in a weekend. Be skeptical of anything promising to "rewire your brain" in days. Think weeks and months of consistent reps, and expect the old pattern to show up now and then. A slip isn't failure. It's one rep, and the next one is yours.

One more step I'd add for the leaders I work with: practice your state. Your emotional state shapes which patterns fire. When you're stressed and depleted, the oldest, most rehearsed habits tend to take over. Learning to steady your nervous system gives the new pattern a fighting chance. The Bigger Picture: You Get to Choose What You Keep

What I love about synaptic pruning is the permission it gives you. You don't have to win a war against your old habits. You can simply stop feeding them, feed something better, and let your brain do what it already does well.
